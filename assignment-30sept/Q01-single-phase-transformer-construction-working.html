1.Transformer: definition

A transformer is a static electrical device that transfers electrical energy from one AC circuit to another by mutual induction. It changes the level of voltage and current but keeps the frequency the same. A single-phase transformer has two windings, the primary (connected to the supply) and the secondary (connected to the load), placed on a common laminated iron core.

2.Construction

The two basic parts are the magnetic core and the windings. Insulation holds them apart. Larger (power) transformers also have a tank, oil and accessories.

2.1 Core

  • Silicon steel: high permeability, so a small magnetising current sets up the flux; low hysteresis loss; higher resistivity, so smaller eddy currents.
  • Laminated: the core is a stack of thin insulated sheets, not a solid block. Eddy currents are confined to each thin sheet (Fig. 3a), and eddy-current loss falls with the square of the thickness: Pe = ke f² Bm² t²
  • Staggered joints: strips are laid in alternate layers so that joints of one layer are covered by solid steel in the next (Fig. 3b). This avoids a continuous air gap, which keeps reluctance and magnetising current low.
  • Limb section: small units use a square limb; large core-type units use a stepped (cruciform) limb so round coils fit with less wasted space and copper.

2.2 Windings

  • The HV winding has many turns of thinner wire; the LV winding has fewer turns of thicker wire, since it carries the larger current.
  • LV winding is placed next to the core and HV outside it, because the LV winding needs less insulation from the earthed core. HV outside is also easier to reach for tappings.
  • Each winding is split: half of LV and half of HV on each limb (core type), or interleaved discs (shell type). Keeping primary and secondary close reduces leakage flux, and so leakage reactance.

3.Working principle

Principle: a transformer works on mutual induction between two coils linked by a common, time-varying magnetic flux, as given by Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction (e = −N dΦ/dt).

3.1 Step by step

  1. AC voltage V₁ is applied to the primary. It draws a small no-load current I₀, giving an alternating mmf N₁I₀.
  2. This mmf sets up an alternating flux Φ = Φm sin ωt in the core. The low-reluctance core carries almost all of it, so it links both windings (mutual flux).
  3. The changing flux induces emf in both windings: e₁ = −N₁ dΦ/dt (self-induced, opposes V₁ by Lenz's law) and e₂ = −N₂ dΦ/dt (mutually induced).
  4. When a load is connected, E₂ drives current I₂ through it. The secondary mmf N₂I₂ opposes the flux.
  5. The primary at once draws an extra load current I₂′ with N₁I₂′ = N₂I₂, cancelling the secondary mmf. Total I₁ = I₀ + I₂′ (phasor sum), and the flux stays nearly constant.
  6. Result: power flows from primary to secondary through the magnetic field, at the same frequency, with voltage changed in the ratio of turns.

3.4 Points to note

  • No electrical connection between primary and secondary; energy passes only through the mutual flux.
  • Frequency is unchanged, because the same flux links both windings.
  • Works only on AC. On DC the flux is constant, so dΦ/dt = 0 and no emf is induced. With no back emf, the primary current is V₁/R₁, which is very large, and the winding can overheat and burn.
  • Flux stays nearly constant from no load to full load, since V₁ ≈ E₁ = 4.44 f N₁ Φm is fixed by the supply.
  • Power (VA) in ≈ power out: stepping voltage up steps current down by the same ratio. This is why high-voltage transmission cuts I²R line loss.

4.Conclusion

A single-phase transformer is built from a laminated silicon-steel core (core or shell type) carrying two insulated copper windings; power units add a tank, oil and protective accessories. It works on mutual induction: the alternating flux set up by the primary links the secondary and induces an emf in it, with E₂/E₁ = N₂/N₁ and no change in frequency.
