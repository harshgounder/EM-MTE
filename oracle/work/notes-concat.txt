# Electrical Machines

## Motors and generators

- A **motor** converts electrical energy into mechanical energy. It is identified from its input.
- A **generator** converts mechanical energy into electrical energy. It is identified from its output.

## Electromagnetic induction

- \(\Phi\) is magnetic flux.
- \(N\) is the number of turns in a coil.
- Induced emf is proportional to the number of turns:

  \[
  e \propto N
  \]

- **Faraday's law**:

  \[
  e = -N\frac{d\Phi}{dt}
  \]

  The negative sign expresses Lenz's law: the induced emf opposes the change that produces it.

- A fixed magnetic field can use DC excitation. To obtain a changing flux, use AC excitation.
- With a static magnetic field, emf can still be generated by relative motion between the conductor and the magnetic field. This is called **dynamically induced emf**.

> Check: the source says that induced emf cannot be DC. More precisely, the polarity and magnitude of an induced emf depend on the rate of change of flux linkage. A steady relative motion commonly gives an alternating emf, but a unidirectional changing flux can produce a unidirectional emf.

## Transformer

A transformer is a static electromagnetic device that transfers electrical energy from one circuit to another without changing the frequency.

- Its efficiency is generally high, commonly above 95%.
- It consists of copper windings and an iron core.
- Its operating principle is electromagnetic induction.

### Step-up transformer

A step-up transformer increases voltage by increasing the number of turns on the secondary winding:

\[
N_2 > N_1, \qquad V_2 > V_1, \qquad I_2 < I_1
\]

For an ideal transformer:

\[
\frac{V_1}{V_2}=\frac{N_1}{N_2}=\frac{I_2}{I_1}
\]

The source's final ratio was written as \(V_1/V_2 = I_2/I_1\); the turns ratio is included above because it is part of the ideal-transformer relation.

# Transformer Equations

## Induced-emf equation

For sinusoidal core flux, the rms induced emfs are:

\[
E_1 = 4.44fN_1\Phi_m
\]

\[
E_2 = 4.44fN_2\Phi_m
\]

Since \(\Phi_m=B_mA\), the primary emf equation can also be written as:

\[
E_1=4.44fN_1B_mA
\]

where:

- \(f\) is the supply frequency in hertz.
- \(N_1\) and \(N_2\) are the primary and secondary turns.
- \(\Phi_m\) is the maximum (peak) core flux in weber.

For an ideal transformer, terminal voltages equal the corresponding induced emfs:

\[
E_1=V_1, \qquad E_2=V_2
\]

## Voltage and turns ratio

\[
\frac{V_2}{V_1}=\frac{E_2}{E_1}=\frac{N_2}{N_1}=k
\]

Here \(N_1/N_2\) is commonly called the primary-to-secondary turns ratio, while \(k=N_2/N_1\) is the transformation ratio used in these notes.

## Impedance transformation

With impedances referred to corresponding sides of an ideal transformer:

\[
\frac{Z_1}{Z_2}
= \frac{V_1/I_1}{V_2/I_2}
= \frac{V_1I_2}{V_2I_1}
= \left(\frac{N_1}{N_2}\right)^2
\]

Thus, impedance transforms as the square of the turns ratio.

## Maximum flux density

\[
B_m=\frac{\Phi_m}{A}
\]

where \(B_m\) is the maximum flux density in tesla and \(A\) is the core cross-sectional area in square metres.

# Transformer Equivalent Circuit, Worked Example

## Approximate equivalent circuit

For a loaded transformer, the no-load current \(I_0\) is usually about 1% to 3% of rated primary current. It may therefore be neglected for an approximate equivalent circuit without serious error.

### Circuit, secondary quantities referred to the primary side

```text
      R₁        jX₁        R₂′       jX₂′
 V₁ ──/\/\/─────LLLL──────/\/\/──────LLLL────── Z₂′
  │                                                    │
  └────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The prime (′) denotes a secondary quantity referred to the primary side.

## Problem

A 30 kVA, 2000/200 V, single-phase, 50 Hz transformer has:

| Quantity | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---:|---:|
| Resistance | \(R_1=3.5\,\Omega\) | \(R_2=0.015\,\Omega\) |
| Leakage reactance | \(X_1=4.5\,\Omega\) | \(X_2=0.02\,\Omega\) |

Find the equivalent resistance, reactance, and impedance referred to the primary side.

## Solution

The turns ratio is:

\[
a=\frac{N_1}{N_2}=\frac{V_1}{V_2}=\frac{2000}{200}=10
\]

Refer secondary resistance and reactance to the primary:

\[
R_2'=R_2a^2=0.015(10)^2=1.5\,\Omega
\]

\[
X_2'=X_2a^2=0.02(10)^2=2\,\Omega
\]

Therefore:

\[
R_{eq}=R_1+R_2'=3.5+1.5=5\,\Omega
\]

\[
X_{eq}=X_1+X_2'=4.5+2=6.5\,\Omega
\]

\[
Z_{eq}=R_{eq}+jX_{eq}=5+j6.5\,\Omega
\]

\[
|Z_{eq}|=\sqrt{5^2+6.5^2}=8.20\,\Omega
\]

The next page continues with the copper-loss calculation.

## Problem

A 200 kVA, 6600/400 V, 50 Hz single-phase transformer has 80 turns on its secondary winding. Calculate:

1. Approximate primary and secondary currents.
2. Number of primary turns.
3. Maximum value of flux.

## Solution

### 1. Rated currents

For a single-phase transformer, the apparent power is the same on both sides:

\[
V_1I_1=V_2I_2=200\times10^3\ \text{VA}
\]

\[
I_1=\frac{200\times10^3}{6600}=30.30\ \text{A}
\]

\[
I_2=\frac{200\times10^3}{400}=500\ \text{A}
\]

Neglecting winding drops, \(E_1\approx V_1\) and \(E_2\approx V_2\). Therefore:

\[
\frac{V_1}{V_2}=\frac{N_1}{N_2}
\]

\[
\frac{6600}{400}=\frac{N_1}{80}
\]

\[
N_1=1320\ \text{turns}
\]

Use the transformer emf equation:

\[
E_1=4.44fN_1\Phi_m
\]

\[
6600=4.44(50)(1320)\Phi_m
\]

\[
\Phi_m=0.02252\ \text{Wb}
\]

The source answer, \(0.0225\,\text{Wb}\), is correct after rounding.

# Transformer Tests: Open Circuit and Short Circuit

The open-circuit test is also called the **no-load test**. Transformer efficiency can be determined from two standard tests:

1. Open-circuit test.
2. Short-circuit test.

## Open-circuit test

Measured no-load quantities are \(V_0\), \(I_0\), and \(W_0\).

\[
\cos\theta_0=\frac{W_0}{V_0I_0}
\]

\[
I_w=I_0\cos\theta_0, \qquad I_m=I_0\sin\theta_0
\]

\[
\sin\theta_0=\sqrt{1-\cos^2\theta_0}
\]

The shunt-branch parameters, referred to the test side, are:

\[
R_0=\frac{V_0}{I_w}, \qquad X_0=\frac{V_0}{I_m}
\]

Rated voltage is applied, usually to the low-voltage winding, while the other winding is open. As the no-load current is small, typically 1% to 3% of rated current, copper loss \(I_0^2R\) is neglected. Thus \(W_0\) is approximately the core (iron) loss.

## Short-circuit test

One winding is short-circuited and a low adjustable voltage is applied to the other, commonly the high-voltage side. The voltage is increased only until rated current flows.

- The test determines the series, or winding, parameters.
- The wattmeter reading at rated current is approximately the full-load copper loss.
- Applying rated voltage would cause a dangerously high current. Only about 5% to 15% of rated voltage is normally needed.
- Because the applied voltage and flux are low, core loss is neglected.

# Transformer Rating, Short-Circuit Parameters, and Regulation

## Why transformers are rated in kVA

Core loss mainly depends on applied voltage, and copper loss mainly depends on current:

\[
P_{cu}=I^2R
\]

Neither loss directly depends on load power factor. Therefore a transformer is rated by apparent power, \(\text{kVA}\), rather than real power, \(\text{kW}\).

## Values from the short-circuit test

At rated current during the short-circuit test:

\[
Z_{eq}=\frac{V_{sc}}{I_{sc}}
\]

\[
R_{eq}=\frac{W_{sc}}{I_{sc}^2}
\]

\[
X_{eq}=\sqrt{Z_{eq}^2-R_{eq}^2}
\]

Because core loss is negligible in this test, \(W_{sc}\) is approximately the full-load copper loss.

Rated voltage and rated current are the full-load voltage and current of the respective winding.

## Voltage regulation

Voltage regulation is the change in secondary terminal voltage from no load to full load, at a specified power factor:

\[
\%\text{VR}=\frac{V_{20}-V_2}{V_2}\times100
\]

For negligible no-load drop, \(V_{20}\approx E_2\), so:

\[
\%\text{VR}\approx\frac{E_2-V_2}{V_2}\times100
\]

where \(V_{20}\) is the no-load secondary voltage and \(V_2\) is the loaded secondary terminal voltage.

The approximate voltage drop is:

\[
E_2-V_2\approx I_2R_{eq}\cos\phi_2 \pm I_2X_{eq}\sin\phi_2
\]

- Use \(+\) for lagging power factor.
- Use \(-\) for leading power factor.

# Transformer Loads, Losses, and Voltage Regulation

## Loss dependence

- Copper loss depends on load current, so it varies with load:

  \[
  P_{cu}=I^2R
  \]

- Core loss depends mainly on applied voltage and frequency. At fixed supply voltage and frequency, it is approximately constant.

## Load types and power factor

| Load type | Typical example | Current relative to voltage | Power factor |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resistive \(R\) | Heater, geyser | In phase | Unity |
| Inductive \(L\) | Induction motor | Lags | Lagging |
| Capacitive \(C\) | Capacitor bank | Leads | Leading |

Unless a question specifies a resistive or capacitive load, a transformer load is often treated as inductive, so the power factor is lagging.

## Practical transformer on load

For a lagging-power-factor load, the approximate secondary voltage drop is:

\[
E_2-V_2\approx I_2R_{eq}\cos\phi_2+I_2X_{eq}\sin\phi_2
\]

For a leading-power-factor load, replace the plus sign with a minus sign.

## Transformer losses

1. Core, or iron, loss:
   - Hysteresis loss.
   - Eddy-current loss.
2. Copper loss.

Both hysteresis and eddy-current loss depend on frequency and flux density.

## Voltage regulation

One common convention is:

\[
\%\text{VR}=\frac{E_2-V_2}{E_2}\times100
=\frac{I_2R_{eq}\cos\phi_2\pm I_2X_{eq}\sin\phi_2}{E_2}\times100
\]

Some texts use \(V_2\), rather than \(E_2\), in the denominator. State the convention if the question specifies one.

### Zero regulation at a leading power factor

For zero voltage regulation with a leading load:

\[
I_2R_{eq}\cos\phi_2-I_2X_{eq}\sin\phi_2=0
\]

\[
\tan\phi_2=\frac{R_{eq}}{X_{eq}}
\]

\[
\phi_2=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{R_{eq}}{X_{eq}}\right)
\]

Thus voltage regulation is zero at a leading power factor with this angle.

# Full-Load Voltage Regulation, Worked Example

## Problem

The primary and secondary windings of a 40 kVA, 6600/250 V single-phase transformer have resistances of \(10\,\Omega\) and \(0.02\,\Omega\), respectively. The total leakage reactance is \(35\,\Omega\), referred to the primary winding. Find the full-load voltage regulation at 0.8 lagging power factor.

## Solution

Given:

\[
S=40\,\text{kVA}, \quad V_1=6600\,\text{V}, \quad V_2=250\,\text{V}
\]

\[
R_1=10\,\Omega, \quad R_2=0.02\,\Omega, \quad X_{eq1}=35\,\Omega
\]

\[
\cos\phi_2=0.8, \qquad \sin\phi_2=0.6
\]

Refer primary resistance and total reactance to the secondary side:

\[
R_1'=R_1\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right)^2
=10\left(\frac{250}{6600}\right)^2
=0.01435\,\Omega
\]

\[
R_{eq2}=R_2+R_1'=0.02+0.01435=0.03435\,\Omega
\]

\[
X_{eq2}=X_{eq1}\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right)^2
=35\left(\frac{250}{6600}\right)^2
=0.05022\,\Omega
\]

Full-load secondary current:

\[
I_2=\frac{40\times10^3}{250}=160\,\text{A}
\]

For a lagging power factor:

\[
E_2-V_2\approx I_2R_{eq2}\cos\phi_2+I_2X_{eq2}\sin\phi_2
\]

\[
=160(0.03435)(0.8)+160(0.05022)(0.6)
=9.22\,\text{V}
\]

\[
\%\text{VR}=\frac{9.22}{250}\times100=3.69\%
\]

# Voltage Regulation from Percentage Drops

## Problem

Calculate the voltage regulation of a transformer for which the ohmic drop is 1% and the reactance drop is 5% of rated voltage, at:

1. 0.8 lagging power factor.
2. 0.8 leading power factor.

## Formula

\[
\%\text{VR}=(\%\text{ohmic drop})\cos\phi\ \pm\ (\%\text{reactance drop})\sin\phi
\]

Use \(+\) for a lagging power factor and \(-\) for a leading power factor.

For \(\cos\phi=0.8\):

\[
\sin\phi=\sqrt{1-0.8^2}=0.6

\[

\]

\[

\]

# Transformer Efficiency and Maximum Efficiency

## Efficiency

\[
\eta=\frac{\text{output}}{\text{output}+P_i+P_{cu}}
\]

For secondary-side quantities:

\[
\eta=\frac{V_2I_2\cos\phi_2}{V_2I_2\cos\phi_2+P_i+P_{cu}}
\]

where:

- \(P_i\) is the iron, or core, loss.
- \(P_{cu}\) is the copper loss.
- \(V_2I_2\) is the output kVA at the operating load.

Copper loss can be written as:

\[
P_{cu}=I_2^2R_{eq2}=I_1^2R_{eq1}=I_1^2R_1+I_2^2R_2
\]

## Condition for maximum efficiency

Transformer efficiency is maximum when:

\[
P_{cu}=P_i
\]

Therefore, for secondary-referred quantities:

\[
I_2^2R_{eq2}=P_i
\]

\[
I_2=\sqrt{\frac{P_i}{R_{eq2}}}
\]

This is the secondary current corresponding to maximum efficiency.

If \(P_{cu,FL}\) is full-load copper loss, the load fraction for maximum efficiency is:

\[
x=\sqrt{\frac{P_i}{P_{cu,FL}}}
\]

Hence the output kVA at maximum efficiency is:

\[
S_{max\,\eta}=S_{rated}\sqrt{\frac{P_i}{P_{cu,FL}}}
\]

At a specified power factor \(\cos\phi\):

\[
\eta_{max}=\frac{S_{max\,\eta}\cos\phi}
{S_{max\,\eta}\cos\phi+2P_i}
\]

Multiply by 100 to express \(\eta_{max}\) as a percentage.

# Transformer Efficiency at Full Load and Half Load

## Problem

The primary and secondary windings of a 500 kVA single-phase transformer have resistances \(R_1=0.4\,\Omega\) and \(R_2=0.0015\,\Omega\), respectively. The primary and secondary voltages are 6000 V and 400 V, respectively. Iron loss is 3.2 kW. Calculate efficiency at:

1. Full load.
2. Half load.

Assume load power factor \(=0.8\).

## Given

\[
S=500\,\text{kVA}, \quad V_1=6000\,\text{V}, \quad V_2=400\,\text{V}
\]

\[
P_i=3.2\,\text{kW}=3200\,\text{W}
\]

## Full-load copper loss

\[
I_{1,FL}=\frac{500\times10^3}{6000}=83.333\,\text{A}
\]

\[
I_{2,FL}=\frac{500\times10^3}{400}=1250\,\text{A}
\]

\[
P_{cu,FL}=I_{1,FL}^2R_1+I_{2,FL}^2R_2
\]

\[
=83.333^2(0.4)+1250^2(0.0015)
=5121.53\,\text{W}
\]

### 1. Full-load efficiency

Full-load output is:

\[
P_{out,FL}=500\times0.8=400\,\text{kW}
\]

\[
\eta_{FL}=\frac{400000}{400000+3200+5121.53}\times100
=97.96\%
\]

### 2. Half-load efficiency

Copper loss varies as the square of load fraction:

\[
P_{cu,HL}=\left(\frac12\right)^2P_{cu,FL}
=1280.38\,\text{W}
\]

Half-load output is:

\[
P_{out,HL}=\frac12(500\times0.8)=200\,\text{kW}
\]

\[
\eta_{HL}=\frac{200000}{200000+3200+1280.38}\times100
=97.81\%
\]

# Parallel Operation of Single-Phase Transformers

Transformers are connected in parallel when the load demand exceeds the rating of one transformer, or when supply continuity and flexible capacity are needed.

```text
      Supply bus                    Load bus
          │                            │
      ┌───┴───┐                    ┌───┴───┐
      │  T₁   ├────────────────────┤ Load  │
      └───────┘                    └───────┘
      ┌───────┐
      │  T₂   ├────────────────────
      └───────┘
```

## Conditions for parallel operation

For single-phase transformers, the essential conditions are:

1. Same polarity.
2. Same voltage ratio, or equal secondary no-load voltages.
3. Equal percentage impedance, or closely matched values.
4. Similar impedance angle, or similar \(X/R\) ratio, for proper load sharing.

For three-phase transformers, the following must also agree:

5. Same phase sequence.
6. Same phase displacement, or compatible vector group.

If the voltage ratios differ, the secondary no-load voltages are unequal. This produces undesirable circulating current even with no external load.

# DC Shunt Generator Power Developed and Armature Reaction

## Problem

A 20 kW, 200 V shunt generator has armature resistance \(R_a=0.05\,\Omega\) and shunt-field resistance \(R_f=200\,\Omega\). Calculate the power developed in the armature when it delivers rated output.

## Solution

Rated load current:

\[
I_L=\frac{20\times10^3}{200}=100\,\text{A}
\]

Shunt-field current:

\[
I_f=\frac{V_t}{R_f}=\frac{200}{200}=1\,\text{A}
\]

For a shunt generator:

\[
I_a=I_L+I_f=101\,\text{A}
\]

Generated emf:

\[
E_g=V_t+I_aR_a
=200+101(0.05)
=205.05\,\text{V}
\]

Power developed in the armature:

\[
P_{dev}=E_gI_a
=205.05(101)
=20.71\,\text{kW}
\]

## Armature reaction

Armature reaction is the effect of armature flux on the main field produced by the field winding. It distorts and weakens the main field flux and therefore affects DC-machine performance. Its components include cross-magnetising and demagnetising effects.

With the usual convention:

- In a DC generator, the magnetic neutral axis shifts in the direction of rotation.
- In a DC motor, it shifts opposite to the direction of rotation.

## Shunt DC motor current relations

\[
I_L=I_a+I_f

# DC Shunt-Motor Applications and Series-Motor Characteristics

## Shunt DC motor application

A shunt DC motor is used where speed should remain almost constant, for example in machine tools such as cutting tools.

For approximately constant flux:

\[
T\propto I_a
\]

```text
Torque T
  │    /
  │   /
  │  /
  └──────── Armature current Iₐ
```

## DC series motor

In a series motor, the field winding and armature are in series:

\[
I_L=I_a=I_f
\]

The back emf is:

\[
E_b=V-I_a(R_a+R_{se})
\]

\[
E_b=\frac{\Phi ZPN}{60A}
\]

Therefore:

\[
N\propto\frac{E_b}{\Phi}
\]

Before magnetic saturation, \(\Phi\propto I_a\), hence approximately:

\[
N\propto\frac{E_b}{I_a}
\]

```text
Speed N
  │\
  │ \
  │  \____
  └──────── Armature current Iₐ
```

A DC series motor must not be operated at no load. The small armature current produces a small flux, causing dangerously high speed. This can damage the armature through mechanical and centrifugal stresses.

Torque is:

\[
T\propto\Phi I_a
\]

Before saturation:

\[
T\propto I_a^2
\]

After saturation, flux rises only slightly, so torque becomes approximately proportional to \(I_a\).

# DC Series Motor Applications and Speed Control

## Applications of a DC series motor

A DC series motor is used where high starting torque is required, for example:

- Electric traction and locomotives.
- Cranes and hoists.
- Starter motors.

The series field carries armature current, so it uses thick wire with comparatively few turns. Its resistance is low:

\[
R=\rho\frac{L}{A}
\]

## Speed equation

\[
E_b=\frac{\Phi ZPN}{60A}
\]

Rearranging:

\[
N\propto\frac{E_b}{\Phi}
\]

Since \(E_b=V-I_aR_a\):

\[
N\propto\frac{V-I_aR_a}{\Phi}
\]

## Speed-control methods

### Field, or flux, control

Weakening the field reduces \(\Phi\) and increases speed. Increasing the flux decreases speed.

### Armature control

Two ways to reduce speed are:

1. Add resistance in series with the armature, which increases voltage drop.
2. Reduce the armature supply voltage.

> Check: the source says "field control, increase the speed." This is true only when the control weakens the field flux.

# DC Motor Torque Equation and Worked Example

## Torque equation

Mechanical power developed in the armature is:

\[
P_{dev}=E_bI_a
\]

Also:

\[
P_{dev}=T\omega
\]

where:

\[
\omega=2\pi n=2\pi\left(\frac{N}{60}\right)
\]

Thus:

\[
T=\frac{E_bI_a}{2\pi n}
\]

Using \(E_b=\Phi ZPN/(60A)\):

\[
T=\frac{\Phi ZP}{2\pi A}I_a
\]

Therefore:

\[
T\propto\Phi I_a
\]

## Worked problem

Calculate the armature torque developed by a 440 V DC shunt motor with armature resistance \(0.25\,\Omega\), running at 750 rpm and taking armature current \(60\,\text{A}\).

### Solution

\[
E_b=V-I_aR_a
=440-60(0.25)
=425\,\text{V}
\]

\[
\omega=2\pi\left(\frac{750}{60}\right)=78.54\,\text{rad/s}
\]

\[
T=\frac{E_bI_a}{\omega}
=\frac{425(60)}{78.54}
=324.7\,\text{N m}
\]

# Induction Motor: Starting, Running, and Slip Example

## Starting condition

\[
N_s=\frac{120f}{P}
\]

At start, rotor speed is zero, so:

\[
s=1
\]

Rotor frequency equals supply frequency at standstill:

\[
f_2=f
\]

The rotor impedance at standstill is:

\[
Z_2=\sqrt{R_2^2+X_2^2}
\]

The corresponding standstill rotor-current magnitude is:

\[
I_2=\frac{E_2}{\sqrt{R_2^2+X_2^2}}
\]

## Running condition

Rotor induced frequency is:

\[
f_2=\frac{(N_s-N_r)P}{120}=sf
\]

At slip \(s\), the rotor impedance is:

\[
Z_2=\sqrt{R_2^2+(sX_2)^2}
\]

## Worked problem

A 3-phase induction motor has 2 poles and is connected to a 400 V, 50 Hz supply. Calculate synchronous speed, rotor speed, and rotor frequency when slip is 4%.

\[
N_s=\frac{120(50)}2=3000\,\text{rpm}
\]

\[
N_r=(1-s)N_s=(1-0.04)(3000)=2880\,\text{rpm}
\]

\[
f_2=sf=0.04(50)=2\,\text{Hz}
\]

In normal running condition, core loss is relatively small because the rotor frequency is low.

P=\frac{120f}{N_s}=\frac{120(50)}{1000}=6
\]

Slip is:

\[
s=\frac{N_s-N_r}{N_s}=\frac{1000-960}{1000}=0.04=4\%
\]

Rotor frequency is:

\[
f_2=sf=0.04(50)=2\,\text{Hz}
\]

### Reference-frame speeds

| Quantity | Speed |
|---|---:|
| Stator field relative to stator structure | \(N_s=1000\,\text{rpm}\) |
| Stator field relative to rotor structure | \(N_s-N_r=40\,\text{rpm}\) |
| Rotor field relative to rotor structure | \(N_s-N_r=40\,\text{rpm}\) |
| Rotor field relative to stator structure | \(N_s=1000\,\text{rpm}\) |
| Rotor field relative to stator field | \(0\,\text{rpm}\) |

> Check: the handwritten page assigns 1000 rpm to the rotor field relative to the rotor structure. Relative to the rotor structure it moves at slip speed, 40 rpm. It is 1000 rpm relative to the stator structure.

## Worked problem

A 6-pole induction motor is fed from a 50 Hz supply. Rotor frequency is 2 Hz. Find rotor speed and slip.

\[
N_s=\frac{120(50)}6=1000\,\text{rpm}
\]

\[
s=\frac{f_2}{f}=\frac2{50}=0.04=4\%
\]

\[
N_r=(1-s)N_s=(1-0.04)(1000)=960\,\text{rpm}
\]
